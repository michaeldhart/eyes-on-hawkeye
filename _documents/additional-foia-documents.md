Data Center Impacts

The rapid growth of data centers, accelerated heavily by the boom in Al has
led to significant and interconnected impacts on local communities, national
infrastructure, and the environment. While they serve as the digital foundation
for the modern world their physical footprint is increasingly a subject of public
debate and regulatory scrutiny

1. Power Grid &amp; Energy Demands:
Data Centers are the most energy intensive facilities on earth. The
international energy agency projects that their electric consumption is
set to double by 2030, driving more than half the total electricity
demand in the United States

e Rising Utility Bills: To Support the massive energy needs of data
centers, utility companies must fund costly infrastructure and
power plant expansions. Studies have shown that these costs are
frequently passed onto residents.

e Grid Reliability: The sudden, heavy load strains existing power
grids raising community concerns over localized power shortages
and brownouts.

e Fossil Fuel Reliance: Many center operators rely on backup
diesel generators, that are tested frequently, and when needed
can run constantly for days at a time. This shifts the burden to
local air quality by releasing nitrogen oxides and particle matter,
while further incentivizing fossil fuel production.

2. Water Consumption
Keeping thousands of servers from overheating requires millions of
gallons of water, creating severe local vulnerabilities, particularly
because two-thirds of data centers built since 2022 sit in water-
stressed regions, or well water.

e Direct Consumption (cooling): Traditional evaporation cooling
can require a single large facility to consume 1 to 5 million

---- page 15 ----

Gallons per day- equivalent to the daily usage of a town up to
50,000 people (twice the size of Belvidere).

e Indirect Consumption (power Generation): The water used to
generate the electricity required by data centers is vastly higher
than is used for on-site cooling. Across seven heavily impacted
States including Virginia, Texas and California, electricity
generated for data centers draws an estimated 3.4 trillion gallons
of fresh water annually.

¢ Community Strain: This massive draw acts as a “Giant straw” on
local aquifers threatening public drinking water supplies, lowering
neighborhood water pressure, and stoking legal battles over utility
transparency.

3. Local Community and Quality of life: Because data centers require
massive physical footprints, their physical expansion into suburban and
rural areas directly alters local environments

e Noise Pollution: Industrial cooling fans and massive backup
diesel generators run around the clock, emitting low-frequency
humming that can reach up to 100 decibels, causing severe sleep
disturbances homeowners.

e Land use: Data center campuses span hundreds of acres,
swallowing up valuable and irreplaceable agricultural land, and
natural landscapes, while destroying wildlife.

e The “job paradox”: While a data center costs hundreds of
millions of dollars to build it requires very few permanent
employees once completed. In Illinois, to get the proposed tax
credit they are only required to have 20 permanent employees,
lessoning the amount of tax revenue the city will benefit from. The
tax credit is set to last for 20 years. For only 20 permanent jobs?

e Electric waste: the hardware cycle inside the center is brief; Al
must be replaced every few years to keep up with computing
demands. Pritzker has a bill that would allow data centers to avoid
paying sales tax on any building Al supplies that go into a data

Data Center Major Environmental Impacts

Electricity Consumption &amp; Grid Strain: Data centers demand
gigawatts of power, pushing global and US energy use to record highs
and raise electricity costs for local homeowners and businesses.
Water depletion: Millions of gallons of water are used for evaporative
server cooling and upstream power generation, stressing local water
supplies.

Waste Heat &amp; Urban Warming; Continuous cooling systems exhaust
large amounts of heat into the air, raising local land surface and
neighborhood temperatures.

Air and Noise Pollution: on site diesel and gas backup generators emit
nitrogen oxides, waste particles, and greenhouse gases, while heavy
HVAC equipment produces constant noise above 85-90 decibels.
E-Waste and resource extraction: Rapid hardware upgrades for Al
compute create millions of tons of electronic waste along with raw
material mining waste.

Data Center Noise Issues

. Main Sources of Noise
Cooling Fans and HVAC: Thousands of server fans,
massive rooftops units, and liquid-cooling chillers run at
high speeds around the clock to prevent overheating
Diesel Generators: Backup power systems produce a
loud roar or rumble during weekly or monthly test runs, or
during a power outage
Vibration and airflow: Air ducts and large exhaust
systems create structural vibrations and constant tonal
drone.

. Why Data Center Noise Upsets Cummmunities
Continuous exposure: Normal sounds like cars or
speech eventually stop. Data center equipment runs non-
stop, 365 days a year.

Low-Frequency penetration: Low-pitched rumbles do
not muffle easily behind trees or house walls.

Health Concerns: Documented community complaints
in places like Virginia, Michigan, and Texas note that low-
level noise causes sleep stress and anxiety. As outlined
by EESI DATA CENTER RESOURCES, local noise
ordinances are built for short term parties not continuous
industrial hums.

Data Center Pollution

Data centers cause pollution through carbon and air emissions from
fossil fueled energy, diesel backup generators, massive water
consumption, chemical discharge for cooling, persistent noise and
light disturbances, and chemical waste like PFAS and electronic
equipment.

According to Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health, fine
particulate matter and air drive severe public health risks and
economic risks in communities.

1. Air Pollution &amp; Climate Emissions

e Fossil Fuel Power: Over 55% of data center energy relies
on coal, natural gas, and oil, driving massive carbon
dioxide (CO2) emissions.

e Diesel Backup Generators: Emergency generators emit
nitrogen oxides, carbon monoxide, and fine particle
matter during outages and frequent regular testing.

e Health Costs: Research highlighted by VCU News shows
data center emissions contribute to thousands of asthma
cases and premature deaths, with public health
projected to reach $20 Billion annually.

2. Water Usage &amp; Water Pollution:

e High Consumption: Al facilities consume 1 to 5 million
gallons of water daily for evaporative cooling, equivalent
to asmall town.

e Chemical Discharges: Cooling systems release
biocides, corrosion inhibitors, and trace heavy metals

---- page 20 ----

such as copper, zinc, and lead into the local water
networks.

e Salinity &amp; Depletion: Evaporation concentrates local
mineral and salt levels, straining regional basins and
agricultural water supplies

3. Noise, Heat &amp; Chemical Pollution

e Noise Pollution: HVAC equipment, large cooling
equipment, create noise levels of 85 to 96 decibels,
disrupting sleep, wildlife and farm animals.

e Thermal Heat Islands: Waste heat from dense server
operations can raise ambient air temperatures in
suburban neighborhoods by up to 4 degrees Fahrenheit
and higher in cities.

e PFAS &amp; E-Waste: Semiconductor, coolants, and fire
suppressants leak “forever chemicals” (PFAS), alongside
accumulating electronic waste (E-waste) from discarded
hardware. Emergency responders need more equipment
and highly efficient training for data centers. Usually, it’s
a onetime cost paid by the data center, however, the
municipalities tax payers will eventually pick up the full
on going coast.

e Unique Hazards: Data centers present high-voltage
electric systems, large scale uninterruptible power,
supplies, backup lithium-ion battery arrays (with thermal
runaway explosion risks), and specialized clean agent or
inert gas suppression systems rather than standard
commercial structures. This will create a high cost for
training and risk to BFD firefighter injury.

---- page 23 ----

Currently Passed onto Constituency

e Right now, tech companies benefit from regular industrial
utility structures, shifting their massive growth expenses onto
ordinary households in two ways.

1. The supply Side (Capacity Market Auctions): Grid
operators like PJM interconnection and MISO hold
auctions to secure enough reserve power to prevent
blackouts. Because data centers require massive, round-
the-clock electricity, their projected demand has sent
auction prices through the roof. Since reserves make up
roughly 20% of the supply portion of the consumer’s bill,
these soaring auction costs are divided up and passed
directly to residential ComEd and American customer,
driving up supply charges by 29% to 50%

2. Infrastructure upgrades: When a 500-megawatt data
center connects to the grids, the utility company must

What’s the environmental impact of data centers?

Unchecked electricity demand from data centers threatens to increase health and
climate-harming pollution, deplete water supplies, and undermine our transition to clean
energy. Nearby communities suffer from increases in local air and water pollution,
electronic waste, and noise pollution.

Al data centers also require immense amounts of water for cooling, often one to five
million gallons per day. Although less water-intensive technologies are available, most
data centers still rely on inefficient evaporative cooling.

Rather than clean batteries, many data centers rely on dirty diesel generators for
backup power, which emit toxic air pollution including nitrogen oxides, particulate
matter, and carbon monoxide. These Generators don't just operate during an outage,
they are tested regularly, as often as weekly.

Current projections for the growing energy demand of data centers threaten to keep
fossil fuel-burning power plants online, disrupting our transition to clean energy.

Utilities and their affiliated companies see the construction of new power plants as a
lucrative opportunity for a guaranteed profit. Our health and climate should not be
sacrificed for big tech companies’ speculative bet on Al.

Will my electricity bill go up because of data centers?

Utilities spend billions to serve the immense energy demands of individual data centers,
often leaving communities footing the bill.

The upfront costs for new power plants with grid infrastructure to serve data centers can
exceed billions of dollars. Generations of customers could be on the hook to pay off
those costs, unless utilities and regulators implement protections to ensure data centers
pay their own way. Illinois has not done that, nor does the pending act.

Data center companies often negotiate secretive, special contracts for discounted
power rates with utilities that shift costs onto regular household utility bills. Data centers
are also raising wholesale electricity prices. In the biggest electricity market in the
country, PJM, data centers caused wholesale costs to increase by $7.3 billion in a
recent auction. They were also responsible for an additional $4.4 billion in transmission
projects in 2024 alone.

Tech companies need to pay their fair share. Households should not be subsidizing
electricity for some of America’s largest, wealthiest companies.

# A town's first data center: what to require before saying yes

A data center can bring construction employment, tax base, and infrastructure investment. It can
also impose demands on electricity, water, public services, and nearby neighborhoods that last
for decades and outlive the equipment inside.

Two facts should frame everything. The industry produces very few permanent jobs relative to
its cost — one watchdog analysis of eleven major projects found subsidies averaging about
$1.95 million per job. And in a survey of data center owners, only three percent named tax
credits and local incentives as the biggest siting factor; land and power availability drive the
decision. You are usually not paying for something that would otherwise not happen.

*The basic rule:** every important promise needs a stated limit, a reliable way to check it, a
responsible party with money behind it, and a practical response when it is broken.

No agreement prevents disputes from reaching court. The goal is to resolve routine problems in
days and to have money and legal authority already in place for the serious ones.

## 1. Give the town its own qualified team, paid for by the applicant

Before making any commitment, retain a land use and utility lawyer, plus independent
engineering, acoustical, hydrological, and financial advisers as needed.

Where state law allows, require the developer to fund reasonable review costs through an
escrow the town controls. The town selects the advisers, directs their work, and receives their
conclusions. The developer funds the review and has no say in its findings. This is routine for
pipelines and mines and nearly absent from data center practice.

Assign one official to coordinate, with a named backup. Keep a public calendar of notices,
hearings, and deadlines.

Verify statutory notice in writing before every action that requires it. In one Virginia county, the
largest data center rezoning in the country was voided by a court for defective notice after
roughly twenty-seven hours of hearings; the county spent more than $1.7 million defending it
and then withdrew. Notice failures destroy more deals than opposition does.

Identify the landowner, the developer, the intended operator, and the proposed financial
guarantor. If the tenant is unknown, that is an unresolved condition, not a detail to be filled in
later.

---- page 47 ----

### 2. Decide whether the site works before you discuss benefits

Study the complete build-out — future phases, cooling equipment, generators, substations,
transmission connections, water infrastructure — not the first building.

Ask: How close are homes, schools, and hospitals? Can the electric and water systems carry
this project alongside growth already approved? What happens to roads, drainage, emergency
services, farmland, and historic resources? What other uses of this land and this interconnection
capacity does the town give up?

Include other committed projects drawing on the same resources.

**Separation distance is worth more than any mitigation requirement.** The chief economic
development officer of the country's largest data center county has acknowledged publicly that
officials would do it differently, and that facilities were zoned too close to homes. Where a
well-resourced operator has installed acoustic mitigation in response to complaints, litigation
over the same noise has continued anyway. Distance prevents the problem. Mitigation manages
it, sometimes.

A community payment cannot cure an unacceptable physical impact. If the site is wrong, no
number fixes it.

## 3. Put each protection in the document that can actually carry it

| Document | Purpose |

IH

| Zoning and overlay standards | Suitable locations, setbacks, height, buffers, general
performance standards |

| Site approval or conditional use permit | Project-specific limits and conditions |

| Development or community agreement | Authorized payments, responsibilities, contractual
remedies |

| Financial security instruments | Cash, escrow, or letters of credit for defined defaults and
closure |

| Utility agreements and regulatory filings | Service, infrastructure cost allocation, reliability,
customer exposure |

| Monitoring plan | Equipment, methods, locations, reporting, verification |

There is a legal reason for the split. Demands for money or property as the price of a permit are
treated as exactions and must bear an essential nexus and rough proportionality to the project's
impacts — and the Supreme Court has held that putting the demand in a legislative act rather
than an administrative condition does not by itself change that analysis. Ordinary regulation of
how land is used — setbacks, decibel limits, use restrictions — is not analyzed that way.

---- page 48 ----

So: performance standards belong in the ordinance. Money belongs in an agreement the
applicant signs voluntarily, supported by consideration, and drafted with counsel who can
confirm the town has authority to charge it at all. Naming a payment a "capacity fee" does not
create the power to impose one.

Record enforceable land obligations, address transfers, and obtain lender subordination in
writing. Recording alone does not guarantee every obligation survives foreclosure or binds a
future tenant.

Do not promise to control electric service that a state commission regulates.
### 4. Set water limits from a defensible supply study

Have the water provider determine what it can reliably supply during drought and equipment
outages, after existing commitments, environmental requirements, twenty years of expected
growth, and an adequate reserve.

Then set project-specific limits on annual volume, peak-day demand, and peak-hour demand.
An annual cap alone is useless — a facility can sit under it and still spike on the hottest day,
which is when the system is least able to absorb it.

Account separately for water consumed and water returned. Only consumption depletes the
source.

*Do not accept "closed-loop" as a water commitment.** Closed-circuit systems can still reject
heat evaporatively, and adiabatic assist consumes water on hot days precisely when supply is
tight. Regulate the heat-rejection method and the metered consumptive number, not the label.

Require a drought operating plan before approval, using the provider's actual declared stages,
stating when industrial cooling demand must decline, by how much, and how domestic and
fire-protection service stays whole. Restriction of cooling load should precede restriction of
households, and that sequence should be written down.

Require separate metering of major uses including cooling, independent verification of the water
balance, published usage reports, and retained raw data for audit.

An excess-use charge is a price signal, not permission. Paying it must not create a right to
exceed a hard limit, and during declared drought the response is curtailment rather than a fee.

Money does not create water.

Any required retrofit should be selected on engineering evidence, not on the assumption that
one cooling technology always solves it.

## 5. Protect neighbors from noise before the equipment is ordered

---- page 49 ----

Noise is now the most common post-approval complaint and the most active area of litigation,
including proposed class actions in Wisconsin and Mississippi alleging sound carrying more
than a mile past the property line and, in one case, a proposed class exceeding ten thousand
people.

The reason ordinary noise rules fail here is technical. A-weighting, used in nearly every local
ordinance, discounts 63 Hz by 26.2 dB and 31.5 Hz by 39.4 dB. Those are exactly the
frequencies chillers and fan walls produce, and exactly the frequencies that pass through walls
into bedrooms. A facility can pass a 55 dBA limit comfortably and still be unlivable next door.
Content below 20 Hz may not register on standard equipment at all.

Have an independent acoustical engineer establish existing conditions over a continuous
baseline period — not a spot check — and model the full campus under demanding operating
conditions.

The standards should address overall level, nighttime conditions specifically, low-frequency
content and audible tones, combined output from all equipment, and generator testing
separately from genuine emergencies. As starting points for the engineer to validate: a
background-plus-5-dB standard measured in one-third-octave bands rather than a single
broadband number; a low-frequency screen based on the difference between C-weighted and
A-weighted readings; and a tonality penalty for prominent tones. These are drafting positions,
not settled thresholds, and they need professional review before adoption.

Specify measurement locations, weather conditions, instrument class, calibration intervals, and
how unrelated sound such as traffic is excluded.

Continuous monitoring is appropriate near sensitive receivers and is not expensive relative to
project cost; let the engineer set station count and locations and obtain real estimates rather
than adopting a number from a template.

A monitoring alert should trigger prompt verification and a required response. It should not
convert automatically into a large charge based on an unreviewed sensor reading — both
because the reading may be wrong and because a charge engineered to punish rather than to
compensate is likely unenforceable as a contract term.

Require commissioning tests before full operation. For persistent violations, require an
engineered corrective plan on a deadline and, where authorized, restrictions on the specific
equipment or activity causing the problem. Operating restrictions are a legitimate tool because
noise output tracks cooling load, but tie any reduction to measured compliance rather than
assuming a fixed percentage cut delivers a fixed decibel result.

## 6. Address on-site generation explicitly, or the rest fails

---- page 50 ----

This is the loophole that has done the most damage in practice.

One state enacted a law requiring large data centers to pay for the electrical infrastructure
serving them and barring utilities from shifting those costs to other customers — and the same
law permitted self-generation. The result at one campus was dozens of gas turbines running
more than 500 megawatts of self-supplied load, in a community already carrying elevated health
burdens, with the turbines unpermitted and litigation following.

Closing the rate loophole without closing the generation loophole converts a utility bill problem
into an air quality problem with no permit attached.

Distinguish emergency backup from routine generation. Cap annual run-hours. Require the
applicable air permits before the certificate of occupancy, not after. Set emissions standards for
the units themselves. Prohibit generation serving primary load, and account for emissions,
noise, fuel storage, and testing schedules.

Any demand-reduction commitment must specify the mechanism: computing stops, batteries
discharge, or generators start. Those are three different outcomes for the neighborhood.

## 7. Protect electricity customers and the town budget as separate questions
Require the utility and the regulator to answer three things that do not answer each other:

Who pays for infrastructure built to serve this project. Who pays if the project cancels, ramps
slowly, or closes. How the project affects other customers’ prices and reliability.

Be careful how you argue this publicly. The evidence is genuinely mixed — capacity market
costs in one large regional market rose by billions with attributable bill impacts, while a study for
the largest data center market in the world found no historical cost shift onto residential
customers, and federal researchers attribute rising rates to equipment costs and an aging grid
as well.

*“The defensible position is risk allocation, not proven harm.** You are not claiming your bills
already rose. You are declining to carry the risk of a stranded asset built on a forecast. That
argument cannot be rebutted by a study, and it gets you the same protections.

Ask for credible demand commitments and appropriate security before project-specific
infrastructure spending begins, minimum-bill and exit-fee terms with collateral, and construction
and interconnection dates matched to actual grid readiness. Note that many states have already
adopted large-load tariffs doing exactly this. Intervene in that docket rather than trying to
replicate it locally.

## 8. Negotiate a public return you can defend in writing

---- page 51 ----

Publish an analysis of expected taxes and contractual payments, net of incentives and public
costs, stress-tested for slow construction, declining equipment values, successful tax appeals,
and early closure.

Keep four categories separate and never count a dollar twice: ordinary taxes; reimbursement of
project-caused costs; financial security for future obligations; and additional negotiated benefits.

On incentives, the evidence is one-sided. Several states that measured returns found they lose
between 52 and 70 cents per dollar spent on data center sales tax exemptions. Four states are
each losing more than a billion dollars a year. Two of the seven signed agreements in the
benchmark gave no local incentive at all, and both projects proceeded. If political reality requires
something, cap it at a stated cost per job, sunset it, and disclose the forgone revenue annually.

Protect the assessed value. When a facility empties, the owner appeals the assessment toward
zero while the school district is still servicing bonds issued against the promised base. A
negotiated valuation floor with a waiver of appeal below it is enforceable and almost never

requested.

A fixed annual payment adjusted for defined capacity and inflation is easier to administer and
harder to manipulate than a share of reported profit — profit can be zero on paper for a decade,
and a facility built for the owner's own use may generate no local revenue at all. But confirm the
town's authority to impose it before designing around it.

Report construction employment separately from permanent employment. Construction is where
the jobs actually are — a thousand or more workers for two to three years. Negotiate prevailing
wage, apprenticeship utilization, first-source referral, and certified payroll there. Be cautious with
local-hire percentage mandates on private employment; they invite challenge, and a town that
took no incentives has given up the usual defense. A training fund the community directs, with
spending controls and measurable outcomes, is more durable than a headcount promise.

## 9. Put financial protection in place before the exposure exists

Construction damage requires security before construction. Cancellation exposure requires
security before infrastructure spending. Operating and closure obligations require security
before operation.

“None of the seven signed agreements reviewed for this guide contained decommissioning
security. Not one.** That is roughly 3,600 acres and 18 million square feet with no funded path
to removal.

An independent estimate should set the amount, including contingency, and be updated as
scope and costs change. Prefer cash escrow or a clean standby letter of credit over a surety
bond, because sureties contest claims.

---- page 52 ----

For letters of credit, the mechanics decide whether you ever see the money. Banks examine the
documents you present; they do not adjudicate whether the operator breached. So specify
acceptable issuing banks, the exact draw certificate and its signatory, a notice-of-nonrenewal
period long enough to act, and replacement security before expiration. Assign a named official
and a backup to the renewal date with independent calendar reminders. The common failure is
not a refused draw. It is a quiet nonrenewal nobody noticed for eight months.

Require replenishment after any draw. Preserve other lawful remedies expressly, while
preventing double recovery for the same loss. One reviewed agreement made drawing the letter
of credit the city's sole remedy, which turned a $20 million protection into a $20 million ceiling on
a project costing roughly $10 billion.

Identify who pays if the project company cannot. A well-known tenant's name on a press release
is not a guarantee. Name the guarantor, verify its financial condition, and require it to sign.

## 10. Approve in stages, and write the closure terms first

Define what each phase may build and operate. Condition later phases on demonstrated
compliance and confirmed infrastructure capacity. Do not rely on future discretionary approvals
if you have already granted the entitlement — a vested right you gave away is not leverage you
still hold.

Then write the ending. The computing equipment inside these buildings is obsolete in roughly
four to six years. The shell lasts thirty. Every agreement in the benchmark was written for a
facility that underperforms; none was written for one that empties out.

Require a closure plan covering equipment removal, hazardous materials, site stabilization, and
unpaid public obligations, with an objective trigger — sustained load below a defined threshold
for a defined period is measurable from the utility meter and is not a matter of opinion. Fund it in
advance.

If the town owns land, a ground lease is worth considering, because reversion is stronger than
any bond. But reversion delivers a building and its liabilities. It is useful only with due diligence,
access rights, environmental protections, and funded closure obligations attached.

Before the final vote, the town should be able to tell residents in plain language: what will be
built, what it will consume, what the public receives, what happens if it fails, who enforces the
agreement, and who pays to take it down.

If any of those answers is incomplete, resolve it before granting the approval that spends the
last of the town's leverage.
